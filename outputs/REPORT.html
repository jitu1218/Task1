Calibrated Probability Scoring — Experiment Report


Objective and data

Compare probability quality before and after calibration. The reproducible synthetic binary dataset has 10,000 rows, 20 numeric features (10 informative, 5 redundant), nominal 70/30 class weighting, and 3% random label reassignment. Observed positive prevalence is 30.31%. Seed: 42. It illustrates a risk/churn-style scoring task; these are not real customers or a validated domain model. Positive class is 1.

Method and leakage prevention

Stratified disjoint splits: 5000 training, 2000 calibration, 1500 validation, 1500 final test. Logistic regression uses a scaler fitted only on training data. Random forest uses 200 trees, minimum leaf size 5. Hyperparameters are fixed in advance. Each fitted base model is frozen; sigmoid (Platt-style) and isotonic mappings learn exclusively from the calibration split. Six candidates share the same held-out sets. The saved scorer Random forest | sigmoid was selected by lowest validation Brier score (0.0615), before test evaluation. Models are not refitted after selection. Test comparisons describe results, not a new selection rule.

Visual evidence


The diagonal represents agreement between mean predictions and observed frequencies. Points above it indicate underprediction of positive probability; points below it indicate overprediction. Histograms show support: sparse bins are less reliable. Empty bins are omitted from curves and contribute zero to ECE. Detailed bin counts are in reliability_bins.csv; per-row probabilities are in test_predictions.csv.

Metrics and interpretation

ECE = sum over bins of (bin count / N) × |observed positive fraction − mean predicted probability|. Bins are [0, .1), [.1, .2), …, [.9, 1], including probability 1 in the last bin. This is positive-class ECE, not top-label confidence ECE. Lower is better. ECE depends on binning and sample size; metrics.csv also includes 5- and 20-bin ECE. Brier is mean squared probability error. Log loss strongly penalizes confident wrong predictions. Both assess probability quality, including discrimination, rather than calibration alone. ROC-AUC assesses ranking, not probability accuracy.
Calibration does not guarantee improvement for every model or metric. Sigmoid learns a smooth mapping; isotonic learns a flexible monotone mapping and can overfit or produce extreme 0/1 estimates. Isotonic may change AUC through tied scores. A model can rank cases well yet attach inaccurate probabilities to them. Use all diagnostics, not just a single low ECE or an attractive curve.

Decision-making example

If false positives cost 1 unit and false negatives cost 4 units, the minimum expected cost decision under that simple two-error-cost model uses threshold 1/(1+4) = 0.20. At p=0.70, the predicted positive outcome should occur around 70% of the time among comparable cases in aggregate, not with certainty for one individual. Calibration therefore supports expected-cost decisions, risk estimates and resource planning. The costs here are illustrative, not business advice or an optimized test threshold.

Limitations and next steps

One synthetic dataset and one split do not establish statistical significance or real-world readiness. No confidence intervals are claimed. In deployment, use representative data, validate temporal/group splits where appropriate, inspect subgroup calibration, monitor prevalence/drift, and recalibrate when necessary. Use repeated validation or a fresh holdout before making stronger comparative claims.
